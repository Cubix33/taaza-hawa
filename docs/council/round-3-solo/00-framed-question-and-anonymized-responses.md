# Round 3 council: solo, software-only environmental hack

Date: 2026-10-04. These are independent first-round advisor responses. Letters conceal roles for the peer-review round. Responses are reproduced from the advisors with only formatting edits.

## Framed question

Choose the strongest idea for WeMakeDevs × AWS Environmental Hacks, October 8–11, 2026. The entrant builds and films alone. The product must be software-only: a phone and laptop are allowed, and the entrant can film their existing household electricity meter, but no custom sensors or other professionals may be required. The demo must show a real event, a decision, the entrant's action, and an independently checked result in three minutes. The current event judges idea and impact, AWS use, usability, execution, and video; project implementation starts when the event clock starts. Past winners had a watch-triggered call, an SRE alarm/remediation loop, and a two-player evacuation drill. HaqCheck lost points with no product footage and weak user fit. Earlier local concepts—hardware Saaf Twin, school PE timing/forecasting/intensity—face a user constraint or failed evidence test.

Fresh candidates: **A. PulseProof** reads the existing electricity meter's LED flashes through a phone browser camera, guides a controlled appliance ON/OFF experiment, and makes a measured evidence receipt. **B. Tap Witness** listens for a dripping tap before and after the entrant closes or fixes it. **C. Cloud Leak** detects and stops an idle AWS resource in the entrant's account, then verifies the state. **D. School PE intensity** turns AQI into a school activity instruction and plan.

Critical competitive fact: [Energy Meter Reader](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=reader.meter.energy.gmail.com.energymeterreader) already counts electricity-meter LED pulses and compares appliance on/off consumption. Pulse counting alone cannot be the novelty. Relevant new research: [meter-digit OCR](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.12617) (not LED recognition), [limits of aggregate-load attribution](https://arxiv.org/abs/1310.7850), [a five-household feedback pilot](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.01309), [Indian household inefficiency](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.17056), and [context-aware feedback](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02719). Do not award an unsupported 10/10.

## Response A

Choose **A, PulseProof, conditionally**. It offers a measured, repeatable before/after event with the user's existing meter and phone. The intervention must be tightly controlled: hold the camera fixed, observe a baseline, toggle one reasonably large appliance, observe the changed pulse rate, and ideally repeat the transition. That shows a change in *whole-home power at that moment*. It does not identify the appliance uniquely, prove long-term electricity savings, or measure carbon avoidance. Report the observed watt difference, pulse count, pulse constant printed on the meter, time windows, and uncertainty. If you add annual cost or emissions, label them scenarios with explicit usage assumptions and grid factor. The NILM limits paper reinforces the need to avoid appliance attribution from aggregate signals. The mobile meter paper concerns OCR of meter digits, not LED pulse detection, so it cannot substantiate this particular vision algorithm. An existing app already counts LED pulses and compares devices: novelty must be the auditable experiment and actionable receipt, not counting itself. Scores for winning potential, not probability: **A 7.5/10; C Cloud Leak 7/10; B Tap Witness 5.5/10; D PE 4/10**. No honest research process can certify a 10/10 winner. Tap audio may confuse drip with background noise and lacks a calibrated flow estimate. Cloud Leak has exact API state transitions, but cost and emissions savings remain modeled, and persuading judges that stopping self-created test resources matters is harder. The **first kill test** is a raw 60–90 second meter recording during one user-controlled, high-draw toggle. Verify pulse constant and count by hand; then see whether the derived before/after difference is stable across a repeat. If the meter is unreadable or pulse intervals are too slow, pivot to C before building the interface.

## Response B

None of these is honestly 10/10 yet. **A PulseProof: 6/10.** It has the best three-minute film: meter flashes, switch appliance off, flashes slow, receipt appears. But the basic feature is already sold by Energy Meter Reader, whose listing explicitly promises camera flash counting and appliance on/off comparison. An app that merely wraps that loop in AWS and prettier charts will look derivative. The meter observes *whole-home* demand, so a before/after drop does not prove which appliance caused it if other loads change; the aggregate-load limits paper establishes limits on distinguishing loads from aggregate signals. Kill test: get two or more repeatable on/off cycles from the user's meter, with enough pulses in each window to distinguish the change; then compare the proposed experience against the existing app feature by feature. If either fails, pivot.

**B Tap Witness: 3/10.** Phone audio depends on pipe geometry, background noise, and tap pressure. Closing a tap proves a tap closed, not that a hidden leak was detected or water saved. **C Cloud Leak: 4/10.** AWS state can be verified, but an idle resource is not necessarily waste; a carbon number is modeled, and this overlaps the prior Beacon winner. **D PE intensity: 3/10.** Its decision is a lookup, impact is modeled, live data may be stale, and the user's own solo demonstration cannot show changed school behavior.

A possible differentiated A is a *controlled household energy experiment*: record repeated baseline/on/off intervals, test whether a demand drop exceeds meter noise, and issue an evidence-backed, replayable receipt with uncertainty and explicit attribution limits. That remains a hypothesis, not a winning claim. The first proof is a raw meter video yielding repeatable measurements.

## Response C

**PulseProof / Meter Witness is strongest conditional candidate, 7.5/10 concept potential; not an honest 10/10 claim.** Scores: PulseProof 7.5, Cloud Leak 7, Tap Witness 5.5, PE intensity 4. Use one household appliance with a steady, large draw, for example a hair dryer, and the existing electricity meter. Distinctive product hook must be a guided, repeated **ON → OFF → ON** experiment with automatic pulse timestamps, meter-constant entry, uncertainty/confound warning, and an evidence receipt containing raw pulse intervals; basic pulse counting plus on/off comparison already exists. Screens: (1) “Find the device raising your bill” with “Start 3-minute test”; (2) camera alignment showing LED pulse confidence, 1000/1600 imp/kWh entry; (3) one full-screen instruction per phase, live pulse rhythm and countdown; (4) result “measured difference ~X W during this test”, intervals/uncertainty, ask user to switch it off if unneeded; (5) repeat OFF phase, confirm sustained drop and generate shareable receipt. Large text, Hindi toggle, high contrast, no AWS jargon, status “camera could not verify” instead of fabricated result. Claim instantaneous electrical load difference, not actual kWh or carbon saved; future savings only scenario estimate. Solo recording shows actual LED and switch interaction with phone plus screen capture. First test: verify the user's meter exposes legible pulses and a known appliance creates a detectable before/after difference in ≤3 min. Main unresolved risk: competitor already offers pulse comparison, so the repeated causal verification and evidence quality must materially beat it; otherwise pivot to Cloud Leak.

## Response D

**Choose PulseProof, but it is not a 10/10 idea yet.** Its advantage is a real, filmable before-and-after measurement using equipment the user already owns. The direct Play Store competitor makes “count meter flashes and compare appliances” insufficiently novel. The product must earn its place through a clear experiment: capture the meter's pulse constant, measure a stable baseline, switch one appliance off, measure again, and issue a receipt showing the observed rate change and uncertainty. It must never claim that the app identified an appliance from aggregate readings alone.

The four-day risks are concrete. The LED may be hard to track under reflections or camera exposure changes. At low loads, pulses can be too sparse for a three-minute demo. Refrigerators and other cycling loads can invalidate an A/B comparison. The meter's pulse constant must be entered correctly. A drop in power is measurable; annual savings and carbon impact are projections. AWS must do something visible and necessary, such as storing timestamped evidence and generating a shareable verification receipt, without turning the app into an AI wrapper.

Build the demo around a high-load, steady appliance and the LED, with manual pulse marking as a fallback. Show the meter, the action, the changed pulse interval, and the receipt in one continuous sequence.

**Scores for winning potential today:** PulseProof **6.5/10**; Cloud Leak **5/10**; Tap Witness **4/10**; PE app **3/10**. None deserves 10/10 before the physical measurement and novelty tests pass.

**First kill test:** film the meter for two short intervals with a steady appliance on and off. If its pulses cannot be counted reliably, or the rate change cannot be demonstrated clearly on camera, reject PulseProof before planning the build.

## Response E

**A PulseProof leads, 7/10 now; potential 8/10 with a reliable live meter demo and a distinctive verification flow. No honest 10/10 yet.** It matches the pattern behind Suraksha, Beacon, and CampusEvac: a visible physical event, a detected change, and a user action that resolves it within three minutes. It also gives the solo builder a real actor and a real endpoint: their own meter. B Tap Witness could be compelling, but microphone drip detection is vulnerable to ambient noise, many leaks cannot be repaired during a demo, and an estimated water volume would be especially weak evidence. C Cloud Leak is feasible and measurable, but Beacon already presented cloud alarm plus remediation, so the story risks feeling derivative. D PE intensity has a real problem but its core action is a lookup and its receipt models exposure rather than verifies an intervention.

PulseProof's weakness is originality: an existing app counts meter pulses and compares appliance states. Differentiate with an **evidence chain**: capture the meter's printed pulse constant, show confidence and pulse timestamps, guide two short baseline/intervention trials while warning about other household loads, and issue a replayable before/after receipt. An AWS service should do a consequential job, such as storing timestamped trial data and analyzing repeated measurements; Bedrock prose alone would look bolted on. Call the result a change in *whole-home power draw during a controlled test*, not measured appliance consumption or verified carbon savings. Test pulse visibility and sampling time on the exact meter first. A high-power ordinary appliance such as an electric kettle makes a short demo plausible; low loads may give too few pulses. If the meter display only shows coarse kWh changes, this idea may fail its three-minute test.
